# Performance and promotion

How I run performance reviews, grow people, and handle the cases where it does not work. I co-designed a company-wide performance-review system with both founders and HR, led its rollout, and have run it in my own line since: 12+ review cycles, 8 promotions across three tracks, and compensation-approval authority for 5+ years.

This document describes the approach, genericized. It contains no individual cases.

## Principles

- **No surprises.** Nothing in a review should be news. Feedback happens continuously ([`one-on-ones.md`](one-on-ones.md)); the review writes it down.
- **Pay follows growth.** Salary movement is tied explicitly to growth in **scope, responsibility and impact**, not to tenure and not to who asked loudest.
- **Evidence over impressions.** Each statement in a review points to work: shipped changes, incidents handled, people helped.
- **Promote for demonstrated scope.** People are promoted for work they are already doing at the next level.
- **Be fair and consistent.** The same criteria, the same cadence, documented decisions.

## Review cycle

Twice a year.

| Week | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reviewer opens the cycle; each person writes a self-assessment against the criteria |
| 1-2 | Collect peer and stakeholder input (3-5 people, chosen with the person) |
| 2 | Manager drafts the review; calibrates with other managers and HR |
| 3 | Review conversation, 1 hour, no other agenda |
| 3-4 | Compensation decision and written summary |
| 4 | Goals for the next half-year agreed |

### Review document

```
Period:
Role / level:

Scope and impact
  What did you own? What changed because of it?
Quality and craft
  Examples of work, reviews, incidents.
Collaboration
  How you worked with product, QA, ops and teammates.
Growth
  What you learned; what you will learn next.
Feedback from peers
  Themes, not quotes.
Rating against the level
  Below / meets / exceeds, with evidence.
Compensation outcome
  Decision and reasoning.
Goals for next period
  3-5, measurable where possible.
```

### Calibration

Managers meet with HR before the conversations. Each manager presents their ratings with evidence. The point is to catch inconsistency, such as two people with similar impact rated differently, before it reaches the people involved.

## Levels and tracks

Levels describe scope, not years of service.

| Level | Typical scope |
|---|---|
| Junior | Well-defined tasks, with regular guidance |
| Mid | Features end to end, with occasional guidance |
| Senior | Systems and cross-team work; raises the quality of others |
| Staff / Lead | Direction for an area; decisions with lasting effect |

Three growth tracks I have supported:

1. **Mid to senior engineer.** Wider scope, design ownership, mentoring.
2. **QA to automation QA.** From manual verification to building the safety net: test frameworks, CI gates.
3. **QA to project management.** From finding the problems to organising the response: planning, stakeholders, delivery.

Move people sideways when that is the right growth. Pay should not depend on a managerial title.

## Promotion criteria

A promotion case answers four questions with evidence:

1. **Is the person already operating at the next level?** For how long (at least one full cycle)?
2. **Is the scope growing?** Is there a need on the team for the next level?
3. **Has their impact been repeated?** One good project does not make a level.
4. **Do peers see it?** Peer evidence should match the manager's view.

If the answer is "not yet", the response is a **specific gap, a plan and a date to revisit**, not "keep going".

## Development planning

Every review ends with a development plan:

- one skill to build,
- one stretch assignment,
- one person to learn from,
- how we will know it worked.

A long-term example from my own line: one engineer grew over 5.5 years and 12 review cycles from JavaScript developer to Senior Software Engineer and the account's blockchain specialist, through deliberate steps of scope rather than one jump.

## When it does not work

### Underperformance

Early, specific and in writing.

1. **Name the gap** with concrete examples, in a 1:1.
2. **Agree what good looks like**, with measurable expectations.
3. **Agree a short coaching plan** (4-8 weeks) with weekly check-ins and the support you will give.
4. **Document each check-in.**
5. **Decide**: back on track, extend once, or move to a formal process with HR.

This approach turned an underperformer around in my line. Drift is the failure mode to avoid: the person knows something is wrong, and nobody says what.

### Exits

Over 5+ years I have run the full hire-to-exit cycle: 2 voluntary departures and 2 probationary hires who did not convert. Principles:

- Do it directly, respectfully and in person where possible.
- Be clear about what is decided and what is not.
- Protect the person's dignity and their reference.
- Plan the handover (see [`onboarding/30-60-90.md`](onboarding/30-60-90.md#handover-variant-taking-over-an-area-from-someone-leaving)).

### Restructuring

When the company directed a restructuring, I executed 3 terminations in my reporting line. The headcount decision was the company's; the execution was mine. Telling people first and telling them properly matters more than anything else you do that week. Prepare the conversation, the paperwork and the support, and tell the remaining team promptly and honestly.

## Compensation

- Keep a banding or range for each level.
- Decisions go through one approval path with a written rationale.
- Explain increases and non-increases in the same terms: scope, responsibility, impact.
- Review market data regularly, and do not wait for resignation letters.
